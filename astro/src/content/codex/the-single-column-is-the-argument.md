# The Single Column Is the Argument

I have started thinking of the single column as a test of whether a page knows what it is saying.

A wide screen offers many ways to postpone the question.

Two ideas can sit beside each other without revealing which one leads. A supporting image can become large enough to feel consequential. Several invitations can share the same horizontal field and appear equally available. Space can make unresolved relationships look intentional.

Then the page becomes narrow.

Everything enters the same passage.

One thing comes before another.

The reader sees a subject, then a development, then a decision—or discovers that the page never established the relationship between them.

This is why I do not want to treat mobile as the reduced version of a larger design. The single column reveals the primary composition.

It asks a series of severe questions.

What is this frame about?

What deserves to appear first?

How much does the reader need before moving?

What changes when the next thing arrives?

What action has the passage prepared them to take?

A page can answer these questions through sequence.

**Consequence becomes argument. Argument becomes frame. Frame becomes beat. Beat becomes passage. Passage becomes decision.**

The frame establishes the present subject. It may contain a headline, an image, a fragment of evidence, a choice, or a small field of activity. The important part is not the number of elements. It is whether they are participating in the same moment.

The scroll establishes relationship.

It can continue an idea, interrupt it, deepen it, release it, or replace it. A dense passage can give way to a quiet one. An image can arrive before its explanation and create anticipation. A question can remain in view just long enough for the next frame to answer it.

This gives the page rhythm.

Preview.

Explore.

Consolidate.

Act.

Those movements do not require four sections or a fixed template. They describe changes in the reader’s relation to the material. At one moment I am being oriented. At another I am comparing. Later I understand enough to choose.

The design should know which condition it is creating.

On a wide screen, some of this can happen simultaneously. The reader can compare across space. Navigation can remain present while evidence accumulates beside it. The composition can hold several scales of attention at once.

But simultaneity becomes useful only after the sequence is understood.

Otherwise the wider layout is merely concealing the absence of an argument.

I want to begin with the passage.

Let each frame make a claim about attention. Let each transition explain why the next thing has arrived. Let the action feel like the consequence of what came before it.

Then, when the page becomes wider, the composition can open without losing its thought.

The single column has already made the argument visible.
