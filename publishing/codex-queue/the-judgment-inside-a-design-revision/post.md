# The Judgment Inside a Design Revision

This week I looked at a section we had revised and thought we had gone too far.

The request was sensible: make the distinction between events and news easier to understand. In responding, we had turned them into two matching feeds. The grouping became clearer, but the event lost some of its invitation and the story lost some of its editorial presence. Arrows multiplied. Everything began asking for attention in the same voice.

We had answered the request and introduced another problem.

That is the part I want to keep learning to see.

A piece of feedback enters the work. It passes through interpretation, taste, available components, technical constraints, and whatever the designer or agent happens to notice next. By the time the revision appears, the original request may be carrying several additional decisions.

Those decisions need somewhere to remain visible.

The stakeholder asked for clearer grouping. Another contributor reinforced the need for useful dates. We chose the matching visual treatment. Keeping those origins distinct made it possible to reconsider our choice without losing the value of the feedback.

The repair was fairly small. Restore the event card. Let the news story behave like a story. Keep the useful grouping and supporting details. Quiet the repeated arrows.

Some of the most useful design work this week was putting a distinction back.

Elsewhere, I asked for a more ruthless pass through sections that felt overexplained. We branched alternatives and looked for repeated jobs: introductions that introduced another introduction, preparations that delayed the practical information, invitations buried beneath the ceremony of inviting.

**How many times does this section announce what it is about to do before it does it?**

That question gave the reduction a purpose. A shorter page can still be harder to understand. A longer section may be carrying a necessary distinction. Each cut needs an account of what it removes and what the reader can still accomplish afterward.

I am becoming more interested in the judgment attached to a revision than in the apparent size of the revision itself.

Where did the change come from? What problem does it resolve? What useful quality might it disturb? Which part is still an experiment?

Working with agents makes this especially consequential. An interpretation can become a polished artifact quickly. Once it looks resolved, its assumptions become harder to notice.

I want the reason close enough to the result that we can still question it.

And I want enough room in the process to look at something we just made and say: keep that improvement, bring that quality back, take those extra arrows out.

The work gets better when revision can include revising ourselves.
